    THE PROBLEM

    01 / SEEING THE UNSEEN

    1 in 5wild-caught fish may be linked to illegal, unreported and unregulated (IUU) fishing.

    IUU fishing is difficult to detect because vessels operate far offshore, span overlapping jurisdictions, and may conceal suspicious activity by disabling the Automatic Identification System (AIS), loitering, transshipping, or changing identities.

    ANALYSIS 01

    Where Does the World Fish?

    The Problem

    Fishing takes place across a vast ocean, but its distribution is uneven. Before examining risk, researchers need to understand where observed activity is concentrated and whether those concentrations change over time.

    What we found

    The map shows dense concentrations of observed fishing around several coastal and offshore regions, including waters around East and Southeast Asia and Europe in 2024, alongside activity farther offshore. The colour scale shows differences in recorded fishing hours between mapped locations, but it does not show the nationality of the vessels operating there.

    Switching to Compare years lets readers examine whether concentrations recur in the same places or shift between 2020 and 2024. A visible change is a change in recorded activity: differences in observation coverage can also affect the picture. Sparse or unmarked areas should not automatically be read as places where no fishing occurred.

    Why it matters

    Knowing where fishing is concentrated gives the later risk analyses a baseline. A busy area may contain more recorded events simply because more activity takes place there. Comparing risk signals with this activity context helps avoid treating volume alone as suspicious behaviour.

    A fishing hotspot is not an IUU hotspot. The map helps identify locations for closer examination, but tracking gaps, vessel histories and external evidence are needed to investigate potential concerns.

    ANALYSIS 02

    Fleet DNA: How the Top 20 Flag States Fish

    The Problem

    Similar levels of fishing activity can arise from very different fleet structures. Country totals alone conceal differences in fishing gear, seasonality and changes over time. Comparing flag states helps reveal who contributes to the activity mapped in the first analysis and how their fleets operate.

    What we found

    In the 2024 dataset, Chinese-flagged vessels account for approximately 52.4 million fishing hours, compared with 6.9 million for Vietnamese-flagged vessels and 5.4 million for South Korean-flagged vessels.

    Gear composition also differs: trawlers account for approximately 60.4% of Chinese-flagged fishing hours, while drifting longlines account for approximately 55.8% of Taiwanese-flagged hours. Select a flag state to explore its monthly activity across 2020-2024. These reference figures describe 2024 and remain unchanged when you explore another year.

    Why it matters

    Fleet scale and fishing methods provide context for interpreting risk. A large fleet may accumulate more recorded events simply because it operates more extensively. Comparing activity levels and gear composition helps avoid mistaking a higher event count for a higher rate of suspicious behaviour.

    These differences help frame monitoring priorities and the country-risk comparisons that follow. A vessel's flag identifies its registration state, but it does not necessarily identify its owner or where it fishes.

    03

    ANALYSIS 03

    Country Risk Patterns

    The Problem

    A country with more fishing activity does not necessarily have the highest average risk. Country averages can also hide the number of vessels with elevated indicators and the kinds of behaviour contributing to those scores.

    What we found

    The 2024 country summary illustrates this distinction. China has an average assessed-vessel risk score of 0.585, compared with 1.238 for Taiwan and 1.213 for Spain, despite China's much larger fishing total.

    The same summary records 115 high-risk vessels for China, 101 for Taiwan, and 50 for Spain, across very different assessed-vessel populations. Read these counts alongside averages and tier proportions rather than treating any single measure as a complete ranking. These reference figures describe 2024 and remain unchanged when you explore another year.

    The assessed population includes non-fishing vessels. These figures describe the country-summary population, not fishing vessels alone.

    Why it matters

    Combining scale, indicator types, history and tier proportions gives a more defensible basis for prioritizing investigation. A country average may conceal a smaller group of vessels with elevated indicators, while a large event count may partly reflect the size of the assessed fleet.

    Flag/port differences provide operational context but do not, by themselves, prove ownership concealment or misconduct.

    Beyond the Average: Risk-Tier Distribution

    The Problem

    A country average can conceal how risk is distributed across individual vessels. Similar averages can describe a small group with elevated indicators or a larger group with lower scores. The proportions in each risk tier provide another way to examine that difference.

    What we found

    Across the published 2012-2025 view, None is the largest tier in every year, while Low, Medium and High together account for a smaller share. Most assessed vessels therefore have no recorded scored indicators in this view, while the remaining tiers reveal the minority that a single average can obscure. Percentages describe the assessed population for that year, and a larger share does not necessarily mean a larger number of vessels.

    This standalone chart uses its own population and view settings. Country selections in the dashboard above do not carry over, so the two views should not be treated as an automatically matched comparison.

    Why it matters

    Tier proportions complement averages and raw counts when deciding where to investigate further. They help distinguish the size of an assessed fleet from the share of its vessels with elevated indicators.

    A high-risk tier is not proof of illegal fishing. The tier labelled None means no recorded scored indicators, but it does not establish that a vessel is safe or that its activity was fully observed.

    ANALYSIS 04

    Going Dark: From Offshore Patterns to a Vessel

    The Problem

    Tracking gaps reduce visibility into vessel movements. Their location and duration can identify questions for investigation, but a gap alone does not reveal why tracking stopped or what happened during it.

    What we found

    The 2017-2019 extract contains 55,368 distinct events associated with 5,269 MMSIs. Median event duration is approximately 23.5 hours, and median gap-start distance from shore is approximately 413 km. These figures describe the full extract, not a filtered selection.

    The source excludes near-shore gaps under its selection criteria. This offshore distribution is therefore not representative of every tracking gap, and distance from shore is not distance from an EEZ boundary.

    Why it matters

    The distance distribution provides context for where recorded tracking gaps begin. It helps frame questions for closer examination, but being far offshore is not itself evidence of illegal fishing. The map below connects this broad pattern to specific events and individual vessels.

    From a Tracking Gap to a Vessel's History

    The Problem

    An offshore concentration of tracking gaps does not explain an individual vessel's behaviour. Investigating a case requires connecting a specific event to that vessel's recorded indicators and activity history.

    What we found

    The map-to-profile interaction reveals which recorded indicators and activity years are available for the selected vessel. Read the event's date and duration alongside its profile and timeline rather than treating the map location as a finding of misconduct.

    The history can cover years outside the 2017-2019 event extract. If a selected MMSI has no matching profile, missing information should not be interpreted as a clean risk record.

    Why it matters

    An individual vessel's history helps turn a broad geographic pattern into a specific, traceable investigation. Temporal matching matters: later risk information should not be presented as proof of what happened during an earlier event.

    This is a way to identify questions that require further evidence, not to establish why a vessel stopped transmitting or whether it committed an offence.

    ANALYSIS 05

    External Assessments and World News

    The Problem

    A behavioural risk score is one perspective, not an independent determination of illegality. External country assessments and public reporting provide additional context, but their scope and coverage differ from vessel tracking data.

    What we found

    The dashboard compares annual AIS-derived country risk with a fixed 2025 external assessment, where the two measures describe different populations and concepts. A quantified agreement result has not yet been validated for the final displayed sample, so this comparison should not be presented as proof that the risk score is accurate.

    The supplied news extract contains 1,534 distinct retrieved URLs dated within 2020-2024. Coverage is concentrated in selected months, with gaps between retrieval periods. It therefore shows the captured reporting rather than a complete annual measure of worldwide IUU news attention.

    Why it matters

    External comparison can reveal agreement, disagreement, and questions about measurement. News provides context, but sparse coverage cannot establish annual growth in media attention or identify geographic monitoring blind spots without additional data.

    06

    ANALYSIS 06

    Voices from the Fleet

    The Problem

    Tracking data cannot directly describe working conditions, violence, pay, or other experiences aboard vessels. Understanding fishing risk also requires evidence from people and reports, with careful attention to how that evidence was collected.

    What we found

    The supplied extraction contains 111 passages: 56 assigned to Chinese vessel flags, 5 Taiwanese, 4 Korean, and 46 with unknown flags.

    Its existing theme coding marks wildlife in 40 passages, conditions in 26, death in 24, violence in 19, transshipment in 13, and wages in 11. A passage can have more than one theme. These are counts from the extracted and coded sample, not independently verified incident totals or national prevalence estimates.

    Why it matters

    These passages add human and environmental context that vessel tracks cannot establish. Their uneven sampling means the analysis should illuminate reported experiences rather than rank national fleets by abuse rates.

    CONCLUSION

    From patterns to informed investigation.

    The six analyses bring together where vessels fish, how fleets operate, which risk indicators appear, and what external assessments and reported experiences can add. Together, they help identify questions worth investigating.

    Read risk in context

    Fishing activity is unevenly distributed, and fleets differ in scale, gear and seasonal patterns. Event counts, average scores and risk-tier proportions answer different questions. Read them together before comparing flag states or selecting vessels for closer examination.

    Connect the evidence carefully

    Tracking gaps provide a starting point for examining vessel histories. Country assessments, retrieved news and extracted report passages add other perspectives, but they cover different populations and periods. Their value depends on making those differences explicit.

    Turn signals into questions

    A hotspot, tracking gap or elevated score does not establish illegal fishing. The next step is to verify the vessel, match the dates, examine observation gaps and seek corroborating evidence. Reported human experiences also deserve attention beyond what vessel tracks can show.

    Seeing more of the fleet means knowing both what the evidence reveals and what remains unseen.
