    ANALYSIS 01

    Where Does the World Fish?

    The Problem

    Global fishing is not spread evenly across the ocean. Most of it concentrates in a handful of coastal and shelf regions, while vast stretches of open water appear nearly empty in AIS records. Understanding where the activity clusters, and where it does not, is the baseline for everything that follows.

    What we found

    Five regions account for the overwhelming majority of recorded fishing hours: the East China Sea and Yellow Sea, the waters around Indonesia and Vietnam, the Northeast Atlantic shelf (Norway to Spain), the Peruvian anchoveta grounds, and the Argentine shelf. The East China Sea alone contains more recorded fishing hours than the entire western coast of Africa.

    Switching to Compare Years reveals a visible dip in 2020, particularly in the Northwest Pacific, corresponding to COVID-19 port closures and crew restrictions. By 2021 the rebound had already overshot pre-pandemic levels, and by 2024 the overall footprint is larger than at any point in the five-year window. Southeast Asian waters show the most consistent year-over-year growth.

    Equally notable is what the map does not show. The Central Pacific, Southern Ocean, and large stretches of the Indian Ocean appear nearly blank, yet satellite radar research (Paolo et al. 2024) has identified substantial vessel activity in these areas by ships that never broadcast AIS at all. Absence on this map should not be read as absence of fishing.

    Why it matters

    This map sets the denominator for every risk indicator that follows. A region with more fishing will naturally produce more AIS events, more encounters, and more disabling incidents simply because more vessels are operating there. Without this baseline, a high event count could be mistaken for a high risk rate.

    The post-COVID rebound is particularly relevant: the rapid return to and then exceeding of pre-pandemic effort levels suggests that any temporary reduction in fishing pressure was structural (port closures, crew shortages) rather than a lasting policy change. The fleet came back bigger.

    02

    ANALYSIS 02

    Fleet DNA: How the Top 20 Flag States Fish

    The Problem

    Total fishing hours tell you how much a country fishes. They do not tell you how. Two nations with similar totals can have completely different environmental footprints depending on whether their fleets trawl the seabed, set longlines for tuna, or jig for squid at night. Gear type determines what gets caught, what gets killed as bycatch, and how much habitat gets damaged.

    What we found

    China's fleet is both the largest and the most diversified. In 2024, Chinese-flagged vessels logged approximately 52.4 million fishing hours spread across trawlers (~60%), squid jiggers, drifting longlines, and seiners. Even China's relatively modest squid jigger share represents more absolute fishing hours than the entire output of most nations on this chart.

    The contrast with other major fleets is sharp. Taiwan and South Korea are dominated by drifting longlines, reflecting their distant-water tuna operations across the Pacific and Indian Oceans, with elevated shark and seabird bycatch risk. Vietnam is almost entirely trawlers, the gear type with the highest bycatch rates and most seabed damage. Norway, France, and the UK are also trawler-heavy, but operate in heavily regulated North Atlantic waters.

    The monthly trend reveals a distinctive Chinese seasonal pattern: fishing drops sharply every summer during the government-mandated South China Sea moratorium, then spikes in autumn. No other nation shows this cycle.

    Why it matters

    Gear composition is not just a technical detail. A trawler-heavy fleet damages different ecosystems than a longline-heavy one, and the monitoring challenges differ too: squid jiggers operating at night on the high seas are inherently harder to observe than seiners working in coastal waters.

    These profiles also reveal which nations' fleets are most likely to encounter each other at sea. China's diversified fleet operates in nearly every major fishing ground, putting it into geographic overlap with smaller, more specialised fleets, and that overlap is where encounters, transshipment opportunities, and competition for stocks become most acute.

    One important caveat: a vessel's flag identifies its registration state, not necessarily its owner, operator, or the nationality of its crew. Indonesian fishers work on Chinese-flagged vessels. Spanish companies own vessels flagged to Belize. The flag is a starting point, not a conclusion.

    Learn about each gear type and its impact ↓

    Gear Types and Their Environmental Impact

    Trawlers

    Drag large nets along the seabed or through the water column. High bycatch rates and can damage ocean floor habitats. The most common industrial gear type globally.

    Drifting Longlines

    Deploy lines stretching up to 100 km with thousands of baited hooks. Target tuna and swordfish but also catch sharks, seabirds, and turtles as bycatch.

    Seiners / Purse Seines

    Encircle schools of fish with a large net that cinches closed at the bottom. Efficient for schooling species like tuna and anchoveta but can trap dolphins and juvenile fish.

    Squid Jiggers

    Use bright lights to attract squid to the surface, then catch them with automated jigging machines. Often operate at night on the high seas, far from monitoring.

    Fixed Gear

    Includes pots, traps, set longlines, and gillnets. Generally lower bycatch than trawling but gillnets can entangle marine mammals and seabirds.

    Pole and Line

    One hook, one fish. The most selective method with minimal bycatch. Common in artisanal and sustainable tuna fisheries. Labour-intensive.

    Dredge Fishing

    Metal frames drag across the seabed to harvest shellfish like scallops and clams. Highly destructive to benthic habitats.

    Trollers

    Tow lures or baited hooks behind a moving vessel. Low bycatch, selective for target species. Common for salmon and albacore tuna.

    Fishing (generic)

    Vessels classified as fishing by the AIS model but whose specific gear type could not be determined from movement patterns alone.

    ANALYSIS 03

    Country Risk Patterns

    Beyond the Average: Risk-Tier Distribution

    The Problem

    Risk averages are misleading when most of the fleet scores zero. If 90% of a country's assessed vessels have no recorded risk indicators at all, the country average is pulled down by a mass of clean records, hiding a potentially significant minority of high-risk vessels. The distribution tells a different story than the mean.

    What we found

    Across every year from 2012 to 2025, over 80% of assessed vessels fall into the None tier, meaning they have zero recorded scored indicators. The IUU risk problem is not a fleet-wide phenomenon. It is concentrated in a small minority. But that minority is not small in absolute terms: in 2024 alone, thousands of individual vessels carry Low, Medium, or High risk scores.

    The share of vessels in the High tier has remained relatively stable over the 13-year period, suggesting that enforcement efforts have not substantially reduced the proportion of the fleet displaying suspicious behaviour. Whether this reflects persistent offenders, new entrants, or better detection over time is a question the data alone cannot answer.

    Why it matters

    This chart is the reason we do not rely on country averages alone. A nation with a low average score but 200 high-risk vessels presents a different investigative priority than one with a higher average but only 10 flagged vessels. The risk-tier distribution forces the question: are we looking at a widespread problem or a concentrated one? For every country in the dashboard below, the answer is concentrated, but the size of that concentration varies dramatically.

    Country Risk Patterns

    The Problem

    The country with the most fishing hours is not necessarily the country with the highest risk rate. China's fleet is eight times larger than Taiwan's, but does that mean it has eight times the risk, or does the per-vessel picture look different? Separating scale from rate is essential for fair comparison.

    What we found

    The 2024 data reveals a striking paradox. China has the lowest average risk score among the top three (0.585) despite having the largest absolute count of high-risk vessels (115). Taiwan's average is more than double China's (1.238), and Spain's is similar (1.213), even though both have smaller fleets.

    This means a higher proportion of Taiwan's and Spain's assessed fleets trigger behavioural indicators. For Taiwan, this likely reflects its distant-water longline fleet, which operates at extreme distances from shore and is historically associated with higher AIS disabling rates. For Spain, the elevated score may reflect its distant-water trawler fleet operating in West African and South American waters, regions where the disabling map (Analysis 04) shows dense gap activity.

    China's lower average is partly an artifact of scale: its enormous fleet includes many coastal vessels with minimal risk indicators, diluting the average. But 115 high-risk vessels is still a larger pool of suspicious activity than most nations produce in total.

    Why it matters

    Any ranking that uses a single metric will mislead. If you rank by total high-risk vessels, China leads. If you rank by risk rate, Taiwan and Spain lead. If you rank by fleet size, China dwarfs everyone. The dashboard lets you toggle between these perspectives and form your own judgment about where investigative attention is most warranted.

    The flag-state vs port-state comparison adds another layer: vessels flagged to Panama, Vanuatu, or Belize but porting exclusively in China or Peru may indicate flag-of-convenience arrangements designed to obscure ownership and avoid regulatory scrutiny.

    04

    ANALYSIS 04

    Going Dark: From Offshore Patterns to a Vessel

    From a Tracking Gap to a Vessel's History

    The Problem

    A cluster of tracking gaps on a map tells you where vessels disappear, but not why. Equipment failure, poor satellite coverage, deliberate evasion, and transit through areas with limited reception all produce the same data signature. To distinguish intentional concealment from technical noise, you need the individual vessel's history: has it disabled AIS before? Does it encounter IUU-listed vessels? Does it spoof its identity?

    What we found

    Clicking a disabling event on the map loads that vessel's full behavioural profile. A typical high-risk example might show a Panamanian-flagged squid jigger with 8 AIS gaps exceeding 24 hours, 15 encounters with previously IUU-listed vessels, and over 200 hours of MMSI overlap with another vessel (identity spoofing). That combination of indicators on a single vessel is far more informative than any individual metric.

    Vessel histories often span years beyond the 2017-2019 disabling event window. A vessel that disabled AIS in 2018 may show elevated risk indicators in 2022-2024, suggesting persistent behaviour rather than a one-time event. If an MMSI has no matching risk profile, it may have changed identity since the event, which is itself a red flag.

    Why it matters

    This is where the analysis shifts from patterns to cases. The map shows 55,000 events. Most of them are noise. The vessel profile is the filter that separates a suspicious actor from a vessel that lost signal in a storm. It is also where the three levels of the project converge: the global map (where), the fleet profile (who), and the vessel history (what they did).

    A vessel that appears in a disabling hotspot, belongs to a high-risk flag state, and carries multiple behavioural indicators across several years is a qualitatively different signal than a single unexplained gap.

    Offshore Distribution: Where Vessels Go Dark

    The Problem

    When a vessel stops broadcasting its AIS position, it becomes invisible to satellite monitoring. The question is whether the gap is random or geographic. If disabling events cluster in specific locations, that clustering is itself evidence of intent, because equipment failure and satellite coverage gaps would produce a more uniform distribution.

    What we found

    The 2017-2019 extract contains 55,368 disabling events from 5,269 distinct vessels. The median gap lasts 23.5 hours and begins approximately 413 km from shore, roughly 220 nautical miles, well beyond the territorial sea and outside the practical patrol range of virtually every coast guard in the world.

    The distribution is far from uniform. Three regions produce disproportionate concentrations: the Argentine EEZ boundary (squid jigger fleets crossing between international waters and Argentina's exclusive zone), off West Africa (foreign distant-water fleets with minimal oversight), and the Northwest Pacific (the world's most intensively fished waters). These are precisely the regions where high-value stocks overlap with enforcement gaps.

    Very few events begin within 200 km of shore. The incentive structure matches the geographic pattern: disabling correlates with distance from enforcement, not distance from land.

    Why it matters

    The geographic clustering of disabling events is the single strongest visual signal in this project. Random equipment failures would not concentrate at EEZ boundaries. The fact that they do, and that the same regions also host the highest-value fisheries and the weakest enforcement, points to a structural relationship between geography, economics, and evasion.

    Clean zones like Australia, Northern Europe, and the US East Coast, where enforcement is strong and VMS requirements are strictly applied, show dramatically fewer events. For Singapore, the Southeast Asian waters show a distinct mid-severity pattern (12-24 hour gaps) relevant to its role as a major port state and transshipment hub.

    ANALYSIS 05

    External Assessments and World News

    The Problem

    AIS-derived risk scores are generated from one data source using one methodology. If they happen to align with independent expert assessments built from entirely different evidence (surveys, enforcement records, policy analysis), that alignment strengthens confidence in both. If they diverge, the divergence itself is analytically interesting.

    What we found

    The IUU Fishing Index (Poseidon/GI-TOC, 2025) scores 152 coastal states on a 1-5 scale using 40 indicators drawn from surveys, enforcement records, and expert opinion, a methodology entirely independent of AIS data. China scores 3.60, the highest of any nation, independently confirming its position at the top of our AIS-derived risk rankings. Romania scores 1.46, the lowest. The broad alignment between two completely different measurement approaches is the strongest external validation this project has.

    The GDELT news corpus (1,534 articles, 2020-2024) adds a media-attention dimension. China-related IUU coverage spiked to 31% of all articles in 2022, driven by the WTO fisheries subsidies debate. By 2024 it had dropped to 5%. This decline does not reflect improvement; it reflects the media cycle moving on. The gap between persistent risk (visible in our AIS data) and fading coverage (visible in GDELT) is itself an insight about where public attention fails to track actual threats.

    Why it matters

    Validation is not decoration. If our composite risk scores had diverged sharply from the IUU Fishing Index, it would call the methodology into question. The broad alignment, despite completely different evidence bases, suggests both are measuring something real.

    The news analysis serves a different purpose: it reveals what the world pays attention to, and more importantly, what it overlooks. GDELT articles are predominantly English-language and Western-sourced. West Africa and Southeast Asia, two of the highest-risk regions in our AIS analysis, are underrepresented in the news corpus. Media coverage is a lagging, event-driven indicator; the AIS data provides the continuous, systematic view that journalism cannot.

    06

    ANALYSIS 06

    Voices from the Fleet

    The Problem

    AIS data tells you where a vessel went and when it stopped transmitting. It cannot tell you that an Indonesian fisher was beaten for refusing to work, or that a turtle was used as bait because the captain ordered it, or that crew went months without pay while the vessel operated 12,000 km from home. The quantitative signals in the previous analyses describe behaviour from orbit. These testimonies describe it from the deck.

    What we found

    EJF interviewed over 430 Indonesian and Filipino fishers who worked aboard distant-water squid vessels. The 111 extracted passages from three investigation reports reveal a consistent pattern: wildlife destruction is the most frequently mentioned theme (40 passages), describing systematic shark finning, turtle bycatch, and the discarding of protected species as routine practice rather than isolated incidents.

    Death appears in 24 passages, violence in 19, and working conditions in 26. The relative prominence of wildlife over violence is itself revealing: it suggests the environmental destruction is a daily reality that fishers mention matter-of-factly, while the physical abuse, though severe when it occurs, is episodic. Chinese-flagged vessels dominate the sample (56 of 65 flagged testimonies). Korean-flagged vessels appear in only 4 testimonies but carry disproportionate violence mentions, suggesting concentrated severity.

    The geographic and vessel-type terms in the corpus ("southwest atlantic", "indian ocean", "squid jigger") map directly onto the fleet segments showing the highest AIS disabling rates in Analysis 04. The qualitative and quantitative evidence point at the same vessels.

    Why it matters

    This section exists because data without human context is incomplete. The AIS shows a vessel disappearing for 72 hours off the Argentine shelf. The risk model flags it as High. The IUU Index confirms the flag state is problematic. But none of that tells you that the crew was working 20-hour shifts, or that shark bodies were thrown overboard after their fins were cut.

    These testimonies are not a random sample, and they reflect EJF's investigative focus. But the same themes recur across years, across vessels, and across ocean basins. The conditions described are structural, not anecdotal. The word cloud at the top of this page, built from these same reports, makes that recurrence visible at a glance.

    CONCLUSION

    From patterns to informed investigation.

    The six analyses bring together four layers of evidence: satellite-derived fishing activity, vessel-level behavioural risk, external expert assessments and media coverage, and firsthand human testimony. Each layer answers a different question; together they narrow from 695 million hours of fishing across five years to the specific vessels, regions, and practices most worth investigating.

    What the data confirms

    Five flag states account for the majority of global fishing hours. Behavioural risk concentrates in a small minority of the fleet. AIS disabling clusters at EEZ boundaries where enforcement is weakest. Taiwan and Spain show higher risk rates than China despite smaller fleets. And the fishers who crew these vessels describe conditions that align with the quantitative signals.

    What the data cannot prove

    A tracking gap is not evidence of crime. A high risk score is not a conviction. The IUU Fishing Index measures vulnerability and response, not actual catches. GDELT coverage reflects media attention, not ground truth. And 111 testimonies from a focused investigation are not a census of global fishing conditions. Every finding in this project is a signal, not a verdict.

    What to do next

    The most productive next step is temporal: overlay 2020-2024 risk indicators with real-time vessel tracking to flag sudden changes in behaviour. Network analysis of vessel encounters would reveal whether high-risk vessels operate in clusters or independently. And the forced-labour network proximity data deserves a dedicated investigation: if the median distance across the global fleet is small, the problem is structurally embedded, not confined to a few operators.

    The ocean is large. The fleet is larger. But it is no longer invisible.

    LIMITATIONS

    Working across uneven ground.

    The datasets powering this project were not designed to work together. They come from different research teams, cover different time spans, and describe the ocean at different resolutions. The only calendar year shared by every source is 2024.

    Temporal mismatch

    The AIS disabling events (Welch et al.) cover only 2017–2019 and do not overlap with the fleet effort data at all. We use the disabling map as a geographic reference for where gaps concentrate, not as a temporal comparison. For vessel-level disabling analysis aligned with fleet effort, we rely on the disabling_over_12_hours column from the IUU Risk dataset, which does cover 2020–2024.

    Public data coverage

    All core datasets are publicly available under CC BY-NC 4.0 or equivalent licenses. This is a strength for reproducibility but a constraint on resolution: GFW's public fleet data is aggregated to 0.1° grid cells and monthly intervals, not individual vessel tracks. Each source was released independently with its own methodology and update cycle, which is why full overlap is limited to a single year.

    Recommendation

    Default to 2024 for any cross-dataset comparison — it is the only year where fleet effort, IUU vessel risk, news coverage, fisher testimonies, and the IUU Fishing Index all have data. The broader 2020–2024 window works for fleet effort and IUU risk trends, but external validation (GDELT, EJF, IUU Index) is most reliable at 2024.
